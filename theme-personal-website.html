Project themes · Personal website

Theme: personal website

A simple page about you, built for a visitor who has about a minute.

  • ForA visitor who has just heard your name
  • You’ll buildOne responsive page with a contact link
  • AI featuresNone needed. AI helps you build it.
  • Good if you likeWriting, layout and visual polish

Please read this firstThis is a personal learning exercise. Use fictional or public sample content only. Do not enter, upload or share any LBG data in AI tools, GitHub repositories, prompts, screenshots or published websites. More on staying safe.

Your actual project starts on the day. This page is a short brief to help you choose a theme. Please don’t start designing or building beforehand. On the day you’ll plan it with AI first.

The problem we’re solving

I looked you up, but all I found were scattered profiles.

A new contact, on their phone

People who want to know who you are, what you do and how to reach you usually find scattered profiles, or nothing at all. A personal website gives them one tidy place, in your words, that you control.

Who it’s for

The visitor

Someone who has just heard your name, such as a new contact, a collaborator or a recruiter. They are probably on a phone and will give you a minute.

You

The person who has to keep it up to date. If editing it feels like a chore, it will go stale.

See a real one

Example

bonhard.ai

The personal site of Phil Bonhard, who organised this hackathon. Open it on your phone and notice:

  • A one-line headline that says what Phil does.
  • A handful of short sections: experience, selected work and how Phil works.
  • A clear invitation to get in touch at the end.
  • A calm, consistent look. This hackathon site borrows its colours and font.

Visit bonhard.ai

It’s a real person’s site. Borrow ideas about structure and tone, then build yours for a fictional persona.

Product considerations

Suggestions to think about, not requirements. Use the ones that help.

  • Safe default: a fictional persona. Build the site for a made-up person, or use public sample content. Being happy to publish something doesn’t make it OK to use: the safety rules still apply, so leave out anything from work, and phone numbers and addresses. Whether you can use your own already-public details, such as a public profile, is still to be confirmed To be confirmed. Until then, use a fictional persona.

  • One clear message. The top of the page should say who you are and what you do in a sentence or two.

  • A simple way to get in touch. An email link or a profile link is enough. A contact form needs a service behind it, so it’s not part of the first version.

  • Phone first. Check it at a narrow width. Text should be readable without zooming.

  • Your voice. Write like you talk. Short and honest beats polished and vague.

  • Easy to change. Choose something you can edit yourself a month from now, and publish it at an address you can share.

How you’ll know it works

  • The address opens on another device.
  • Text is readable on a narrow screen.
  • Navigation and the contact link work.
  • No unfinished placeholder sections.

If you finish early, stretch ideas

  • Project pages. Detail pages for individual projects.

  • Light and dark. A light and dark theme switcher.

  • Richer interactions. Small animations, or a filterable list of work.

  • Analytics. Optional, and think about privacy first.

  • A contact form. With a service that handles it.

What to avoid

  • Placeholder text or empty sections. Cut a section rather than leave it half done.
  • Anything confidential, or anything about colleagues or your employer.
  • A contact form, payments or sign-in in the first version.
  • Spending the day on visual polish before the content is written.
